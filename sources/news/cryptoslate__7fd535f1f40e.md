Make CryptoSlate preferred on Share Share The US Senate has abandoned plans to vote on the landmark CLARITY Act legislation before its August break. On Aug. 6, Senate Majority Leader John Thune confirmed that the legislation will have to wait until lawmakers return Sept. 14, reversing his earlier expectation that the Senate would act before leaving Washington. A spokesperson for the lawmaker said on X: “The Dems are insistent on no Clarity vote&#8230; I worked with sponsors of the bill&#8230;and we’re getting that queued up first thing when we come back.” The retreat caps weeks of pressure from crypto executives and Republican lawmakers who had pushed for senators to vote before the recess. Consequently, prediction traders have marked down the bill's prospects for this year. A Polymarket contract on whether CLARITY will be signed into law by the end of 2026 put the probability at about 14%, after odds had risen above 80% earlier this year. CLARITY Act Odds of Passage in 2026 (Source: Polymarket) Why did the CLARITY Act stall? CLARITY stalled because Republican negotiators could not resolve the banking, ethics, and illicit-finance disputes needed to assemble 60 Senate votes to advance the legislation. One complication emerged from within their own ranks as Sen. Jerry Moran of Kansas indicated he wants changes favored by banks before supporting the legislation, making the path to 60 votes more difficult. Over the past few months, banks have been pushing lawmakers to restrict rewards that crypto exchanges and other intermediaries can pay customers for holding stablecoins. The GENIUS Act already bars stablecoin issuers from directly paying interest or yield, but banking groups argue that exchanges could circumvent that restriction by offering similar rewards themselves. They contend such products could pull deposits from traditional banks and reduce money available for lending. Crypto companies argue that extending the restriction would protect banks from competition. Sens. Thom Tillis and Angela Alsobrooks attempted to bridge the divide in May with language distinguishing passive rewards from incentives tied to payments and other activity. However, banking groups said the compromise did not go far enough, leaving the issue unresolved as senators approached the recess. Meanwhile, the Democrats needed to reach the 60-vote threshold have also highlighted separate concerns with the bill. Several Democratic lawmakers are demanding stronger safeguards against money laundering, sanctions evasion and other illicit uses of digital assets. They are also seeking restrictions on senior government officials profiting from crypto businesses, an issue sharpened by the Trump family's extensive involvement in the industry. A bipartisan ethics proposal sent to the White House would require Trump to divest from crypto-related businesses, Bloomberg reported. But the proposal could also allow him to defer capital gains taxes associated with the divestment, adding another complication to negotiations. Sen. Chris Van Hollen captured the broader Democratic resistance this week when he said the CLARITY Act was “not ready for prime time,” pointing to unresolved financial-stability and illicit-finance concerns. September leaves CLARITY little room for another miss Those unresolved disputes will now have to be settled against a calendar that offers senators little room for another failed attempt. The Senate reconvenes Sept. 14 but is scheduled to be away again Sept. 21. Another state work period begins Oct. 5 and runs through Nov. 6, leaving roughly 14 scheduled weekdays in Washington before lawmakers scatter for the final stretch of the midterm campaign. CLARITY will also have to compete for floor time with government funding, nominations and other unfinished legislation. Even securing those 60 votes during that window would not finish the process, as any Senate version that differs from the measure already passed by the House would have to be reconciled before final legislation could reach Trump's desk.
