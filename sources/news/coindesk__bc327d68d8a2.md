(Claudio Schwarz/Unsplash) Summary Show The Fed held rates at 3.5%-3.75% for a fifth straight meeting, but a 9-3 vote and Warsh&#x27;s hawkish tone caught some off guard. Analysts agree the tone was hawkish but split on what it means next. Bitcoin held near $64,000 through the decision, even as stocks slid and Treasury yields rose. The Fed held interest rates steady Wednesday, in a decision shadowed by unusual uncertainty after several prominent voices had called for a hike. Bitcoin BTC $ 63,913.58 barely blinked, trading in a tight band around $64,000 through the decision and Chair Kevin Warsh&#x27;s press conference. Despite the calm, analysts appear divided on the post-Fed outlook for BTC. Some are calling the Fed’s decision the least favorable outcome for digital assets, while others are taking a more constructive view. Others emphasize that bitcoin’s outlook depends on what happens with other assets, with some saying the real test for bitcoin still lies ahead at the September Fed meeting. Andrei Grachev, managing partner at DWF Labs, is worried about what Wednesday&#x27;s hold, delivered with a hawkish tone, signals about the Fed&#x27;s tolerance for pain. The hawkishness wasn&#x27;t subtle. The Federal Open Market Committee held rates at 3.5%-3.75%, but three policymakers – Cleveland Fed president Beth Hammack, Minneapolis Fed president Neel Kashkari, and Dallas Fed president Lorie Logan – dissented in favor of a hike, pushing the decision through on a 9-3 vote. Warsh then opened his press conference saying &quot;there is no soft inflation target,&quot; reiterating that any inflation print above 2% is unacceptable to him. &quot;This is the Fed telling markets it will not tolerate inflation above target even at the cost of a growth scare,&quot; Grachev said. &quot;For digital assets, that&#x27;s the least favorable outcome on the table this cycle.&quot; His reasoning simple. &quot;Tighter policy, less liquidity, [means] more expensive carry.&quot; Tighter liquidity makes leveraged and carry-funded crypto positions more expensive to hold, which can pressure bitcoin&#x27;s price. Grachev expects the shift in positioning to happen immediately, not gradually. &quot;Institutional positioning should shift defensive immediately, and risk-on assets will take the biggest hit, he said. He gave bitcoin some credit for resilience so far, but not much comfort looking ahead: &quot;Bitcoin has held up through a hawkish stretch already, but a fresh hawkish surprise would negatively impact prices.&quot; Can-Luca Köymen, investment strategist at Sygnum Bank, took a nearly opposite view, largely because he&#x27;d already priced in the hawkish hold. &quot;This was broadly the outcome we expected,&quot; he said. &quot;Our base case was a hold, and hawkish language accompanying it is consistent with a committee that wants to preserve optionality while the energy picture remains unsettled.&quot; For bitcoin specifically, Köymen stressed that a restrictive Fed isn&#x27;t the same thing as a deteriorating backdrop. &quot;The signal is that the macro backdrop stays restrictive for a while longer rather than that it deteriorates,&quot; he said. His firm&#x27;s constructive stance on crypto, he added, was never built on the Fed cutting rates soon in the first place, but rests “on inflationary pressure remaining manageable,&quot; and Wednesday didn&#x27;t change that math. “What we continue to watch is the oil path and whether the recent improvement in ETF flows and on-chain accumulation persists,” he said. Bitget’s Chief analyst Ryan Lee’s comments center less on bitcoin directly and more on the assets he expects to absorb the immediate pressure: Nasdaq and gold. Per Lee, the Fed&#x27;s hawkish tone made sense once you account for oil. June&#x27;s soft inflation print, he argued, &quot;was flattered by energy prices that reversed hard on the Hormuz disruption, and the committee clearly knows the July data will carry that premium.&quot; In his view, the debate has already moved past whether the Fed cuts this year. “The debate has shifted to whether the next move is a hike,” Lee said. He offered some encouragement for the bulls too, saying buyers haven&#x27;t disappeared. &quot;Institutional demand continued to absorb much of the initial volatility, suggesting investors are still willing to buy into weakness,&quot; he said, a sign that dip-buying discipline hasn&#x27;t broken down. But he expected the pain, if it comes, to concentrate first in rate-sensitive tech. &quot;The Nasdaq 100 is likely to bear the brunt of that repricing as higher-for-longer yields continue to pressure growth valuations,&quot; he said, adding that gold &quot;could also come under pressure if rising yields and a stronger dollar outweigh safe-haven demand&quot; — a call that didn&#x27;t play out Wednesday, when gold actually closed up 0.27% at $4,048.99.
