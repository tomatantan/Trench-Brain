(Logan Voss/Unsplash) Summary Show Crypto startups raised $11.2 billion in the first half of 2026, with all disclosed funding flowing to regulated, permissioned businesses rather than the permissionless projects that once defined the industry. Payments and stablecoins, prediction markets, and exchanges and trading platforms drew the most capital, much of it from major Wall Street and global financial institutions that are prioritizing licensed, compliant ventures. Investors and founders increasingly view regulatory licenses as scarce, defensible assets that confer competitive advantage, even as retail users continue to trade largely on unlicensed or alternative venues outside the main funding flows. In the first six months of 2026, the crypto industry raised $11.2 billion. Not one dollar of it went to the permissionless, ungoverned experiments that digital assets were supposed to be built on. &quot;There is an irony at the heart of crypto, and it took an $11.2 billion dataset to make it obvious,&quot; said Dubai-based crypto lawyer Irina Heaver, founder of NeosLegal. &quot;The industry was born on a single promise: permissionless. Money and markets that answer to no gatekeeper.&quot; Heaver and her team gathered data that might, as he put it, indicate that “crypto’s permissionless era is over.” NeosLegal tracked every disclosed crypto funding round between January and June 2026. A total of 377 financing rounds took place, Heaver said via Telegram. The top three sectors by capital raised were payments and stablecoins at $3.7 billion, prediction markets at $2 billion and crypto exchanges and trading platforms at $1.7 billion. All three require regulatory approval to operate, she noted. &quot;The money has stopped chasing permissionless,&quot; Heaver said. &quot;It is chasing regulated businesses now.&quot; Prediction markets took point Prediction markets drove the point. Kalshi raised $1 billion in May in a round that included Sequoia Capital, Morgan Stanley, Ark Invest, and Andreessen Horowitz (a16z), among others. Polymarket raised $600 million from Intercontinental Exchange (ICE), the company that owns the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE). Prediction markets pulled in capital in every single month of the first half of 2026 — a total of 34 rounds in six months, she added. And then take a “look at whose names are on the checks, and the story sharpens,” Heaver suggested. BlackRock, Apollo, HSBC, BNP Paribas, Citadel, Goldman Sachs, and Nasdaq all invested in regulated crypto companies, she noted. Mastercard paid $1.8 billion to acquire the stablecoin payments company BVNK outright. Abu Dhabi&#x27;s sovereign wealth fund, ADIA, backed a $355 million institutional blockchain round in Canton Network alongside a16z, Apollo, and HSBC, she said. Rob Hadick, general partner at Dragonfly, itself an investor in Rain, which raised $250 million in the period, put it in terms of direction rather than regulation. &quot;If you look at where the money is flowing in crypto, it is going towards the future of finance and markets,&quot; Hadick said in a Telegram conversation. He explained that Polymarket is building real price discovery on world events, and Rain is driving mainstream adoption of dollar-based stablecoins. &quot;The industry has matured, and the funding environment reflects that.&quot; The value of compliance Vineet Budki, managing partner at Sigma Capital, went into the mechanics in more detail. &quot;Licensing has moved from a footnote to a line item in how we value a business,&quot; he said via Telegram as well. &quot;Code can be forked over a weekend; a VARA license or a MiCA passport takes anywhere between 18 to 24 months and millions of dollars before a project goes to market and processes a single transaction. We&#x27;re not paying for the product anymore: we&#x27;re paying for the years the next competitor loses trying to catch up.&quot; ​However, Budki disagreed with one reading of the data. &quot;This isn&#x27;t a regulation trade, it&#x27;s a revenue trade,&quot; he said. &quot;Regulation is simply the entry ticket.&quot; Gracy Chen, CEO of Bitget, offered the clearest counterpoint. Institutional capital chased licenses, she said, but that tells only half the story. &quot;What funding data cannot show is where users are,&quot; Chen said via Telegram. &quot;On our own tokenized equities, 95% of volume comes from individuals trading a few hundred dollars at a time, 24/7, largely outside the venues that raised the money. Institutional capital and retail demand are moving to different places.&quot; Heaver explained that her research methodology counted undisclosed rounds as zero, so the $11.2 billion total understates actual activity. Six months is also just a short overview, she said, and Budki agreed. &quot;One half-year is a snapshot,&quot; he said. &quot;Three in a row is a market structure.&quot; For founders who are still thinking of licensing as a compliance cost, Heaver has a few words she believes should be taken seriously. &quot;The winning move is no longer &#x27;permissionless,&#x27;&quot; she said. &quot;It is &#x27;licensed, in the right jurisdiction.&#x27; Your regulated status is not a compliance cost.
