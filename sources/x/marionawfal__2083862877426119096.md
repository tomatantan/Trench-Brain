The geopolitical analyst who predicted deglobalization years ago says we're now watching it happen in real time.

Peter Zeihan is known for arguing that geography and demographics, not politics, drive the rise and fall of nations. His predictions are controversial, but they've made him one of the most influential voices in geopolitical forecasting.

His core argument is that globalization was never a natural economic phenomenon. It was a security arrangement created after World War II, with the U.S Navy protecting shipping lanes and Washington guaranteeing the security of its allies. That stability allowed countries to specialize, build fragile supply chains, and trade as if geography no longer mattered.

But as America becomes less willing to act as the world's maritime policeman, the system begins to fray, and the Red Sea is just one example.

The Houthis are a relatively small militant group, yet in 2023 they disrupted one of the world's busiest trade routes, forcing shipping companies to reroute vessels around Africa, adding time, cost, and risk to global supply chains.

Every time a chokepoint closes, a convoy needs naval escorts, or insurers decide a sea lane is too risky to use.

What started as a one-off situation with the Houthis is now playing out in the Strait of Hormuz too.

If history is anything to go by, these won't be isolated incidents, but the new normal, with other countries seeking to use shipping lanes as leverage.

If that happens, it's hard to see how globalization has a future.
