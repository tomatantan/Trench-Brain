Canada’s Ukraine-First Sanctions Crusade is Getting Out of Control

Not sanctioned by U.S. Cleared by other Five Eyes allies. Yet still targeted in Canada.

Why? Because Ukrainian special interests keep pushing, and Canadian politicians keep playing along.

When sanctions survive after the evidence and allies have moved on, it starts looking less like foreign policy and more like political persecution by proxy.

An ethics complaint alleges MP Heather McPherson misled Parliament about a sanctioned individual, portraying him as U.S.-sanctioned and tied to Russia’s war when, according to the complaint, neither was true.

Why does that matter? Because accusations made under the protection of Parliament can have very real consequences: frozen assets, blocked transactions and potentially millions of dollars caught in Canada’s sanctions machinery.

The complaint is not proof of misconduct. But it raises an explosive question: Are Canadian politicians stretching the facts, and the sanctions system itself, to satisfy the most aggressive elements of the pro-Ukraine lobby?
If an MP makes a serious accusation from the floor of Parliament, Canadians deserve evidence, accuracy and accountability. A “rules-based order” means very little when politicians appear willing to bend the rules for their favored cause.

And the consequences may now extend far beyond one sanctioned entity.

While a Middle East conflict puts global energy security under pressure and China tightens its grip on strategic minerals, the Carney government is reportedly standing in the way of American-backed efforts involving new oil and gas supply routes that would reduce dependence on the Strait of Hormuz, as well as projects involving critical minerals across the former Soviet region. 

One entity in particular on the Canadian sanctions list appears on no other Western country’s lists, which is exactly the kind of supplier the West desperately needs.

At some point, Ottawa’s “Ukraine First” foreign policy stops looking principled and starts looking self-defeating. In 2026, that isn’t just bad politics, but a strategic liability for the entire West.
