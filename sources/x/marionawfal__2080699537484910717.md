🇷🇺 Russia’s decision to suspend government bond auctions is a clearer signal of stress than most headlines admit.

Despite a still-low official debt-to-GDP ratio (around 20%), the Kremlin can no longer reliably sell its own debt at prices it finds acceptable. 

Multiple consecutive failed or near-failed OFZ auctions forced the Finance Ministry to pause sales “to stabilize the market.” 

That is not normal for a functioning sovereign borrower, but instead a textbook late-stage war-economy distortion. 

Massive military spending has crowded out productive investment, forced the central bank into an impossible balancing act between high rates (to fight inflation) and the need to keep the war machine funded, and destroyed price signals. 

Inflation has re-accelerated (back near 6% and rising) largely because energy infrastructure is being hit and the state is printing and spending far beyond what the real economy can support. 

Capital is being destroyed or misallocated into munitions instead of consumer goods and capital goods. 

Bond buyers are, rationally I might add, fleeing that environment.

Economic freedom metrics reinforce the picture: Russia scores poorly on rule of law, property rights, government integrity, and monetary freedom. 

It's a government that juggles large deficits, capital controls via sanctions isolation, and a distorted domestic banking system since these are the main forced buyers of government debt.

The eventual result? Exactly what we are seeing: domestic institutions lose the capacity or willingness to absorb more paper.

The Kremlin must now rely even more heavily on the central bank, residual National Wealth Fund assets, higher taxation, or further financial repression of Russian banks and citizens. 

Each of those options either accelerates inflation, drains future capacity, or both. 

Low formal debt levels become irrelevant when the market refuses to refinance you at any reasonable price.

China, Russia’s most important remaining partner, is simultaneously wrestling with its own deep structural problems: 

A collapsing property sector, massive local government debt, and chronically weak domestic demand,which limits how much economic oxygen Beijing can realistically provide to Moscow.

This does not mean imminent formal default...  YET!

It does mean the cost of continuing the war is rising in real economic terms faster than official statistics show, and the room for maneuver is shrinking. 

War economies can run on coercion and money printing for a long time, until the distortions compound enough that even the coerced buyers walk away. 

The failed auctions suggest that point is much closer than the Kremlin would like.
