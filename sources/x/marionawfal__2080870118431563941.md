Money is growing 2–3× faster than the real economy…  and the gap is the widest in modern history.

Since January 2004: 

Canada: M2 +368% vs. nominal GDP +159%
United States: M2 +279% vs. GDP +171%
France: M2 +258% vs. GDP +84%
Euro Area: M2 +211% vs. GDP +102%
Japan: M2 +90% vs. GDP +25%

In every major developed economy, the stock of money has outrun the production of goods and services, often by a factor of two or more.

This isn’t a one-off, but the cumulative result of 2 decades of policy: near-zero rates, repeated quantitative easing after 2008, and the unprecedented 2020-2021 stimulus wave. 

Central banks flooded the system with liquidity, governments ran historic deficits, banks expanded credit, and the result is a permanent monetary overhang.

Some economists downplay it, arguing that velocity of money has collapsed and financialization absorbs the excess into assets rather than consumer prices, but it could be a structural red flag: when money grows persistently faster than output, the excess has to go somewhere, asset inflation (housing, stocks, gold), currency debasement, or eventual consumer inflation once spending behavior shifts.

Japan is the extreme test case: massive money growth, almost no GDP growth, and decades of near-zero inflation. 

Canada is the opposite extreme: the widest gap and the most aggressive household leverage and housing boom.

Whatever your interpretation, the arithmetic is unambiguous. The gap between money creation and economic growth has never been this wide for this long across the developed world.

The measuring stick itself is expanding faster than the things it’s supposed to measure.
